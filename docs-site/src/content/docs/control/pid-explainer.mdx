This page explains how [`Pid`](/control/pid/) turns an error into a
command, one term at a time: proportional, then derivative, then integral.
Two refinements a flight controller cannot do without come after that:
filtering the derivative, and anti-windup.

Every animation plays a trace recorded from the controller's own C++, and
the scenario is linked under each player. The three widgets with sliders
rerun that C++, compiled to WebAssembly, in your browser. The plants the
controller drives are simple models, the same ones its closed-loop tests
use. The controls and keys are described on the
[Trace Player](/standards/trace-player/#player-behaviour) page.

## The loop

The first three sections fly a height loop. The plant is a double
integrator, $\ddot z = u + d$. The command $u$ is an acceleration, and
$d = -3$ m/s² is an offset the controller does not know about: the vehicle
is heavier than the controller believes. The controller sees the height
$z$, and its setpoint is $r = 1$ m. It runs at 1 kHz.

The controller computes

$$
u = K_p\,e + K_i \int e\,dt - K_d\,\dot z, \qquad e = r - z
$$

and the three runs below add one term at a time.

## 1. Proportional: push in proportion to the error

The P term pushes toward the setpoint with a force proportional to the
distance from it. That is a spring.

*Before you play:* a spring with nothing to slow it down. Does the height
settle at 1 m?

Two problems show. The loop does not settle, and it is centred in the wrong
place. The next two terms fix one each.

## 2. Derivative: damp the motion

The D term pushes against the vehicle's speed, like a shock absorber. Pid
takes the rate of the *measurement*, $-K_d\,\dot z$, rather than of the
error. While the setpoint is constant the two are equal, but a jump in the
setpoint does not make the command jump. This is called derivative on
measurement.

*Before you play:* with damping, the height settles. Where?

<TracePlayer
  trace={terms}
  title="What does damping add, and what does it leave?"
  description="The same start, with a D term added (k_d = 8 /s). The P-only run swings as before. The PD run rises without overshoot and settles within about 2 s. The check under the player compares the PD height at 10 s with the closed-form offset r + d/k_p."
  plots={[
    { channel: 'height', title: 'Height', components: [0, 1], labels: ['P', 'PD'], colors: [1, 2],

The gains here give a critically damped loop: $s^2 + K_d s + K_p$ with
$K_d^2 = 4K_p$. A smaller $K_d$ lets it overshoot, a larger one makes it
slower.

## 3. Integral: remove the offset

The I term adds up the error over time. While any error remains, the
integral keeps growing, so the only place the loop can come to rest is
where the error is zero. There, the integral alone supplies the 3 m/s² the
P term had to hold up with an error.

*Before you play:* how large does the integral grow?

The integral makes the loop slower to settle and adds overshoot, the price
of removing the offset. It is also what can make the loop unstable. The
characteristic polynomial is $s^3 + K_d s^2 + K_p s + K_i$, which is stable
only while $K_d K_p > K_i$. With $K_d = 0$ the PID run swings wider and
wider. At $K_i = 30$ with $K_d = 1$ it swings too, between −0.9 and 2.2 m,
with its command hitting the ±20 m/s² limit, while the PD run settles. The
checks under the player then fail, which is the point: the gains are
outside what the loop tolerates.

## 4. Filter the derivative

A real sensor is noisy, and the D term differentiates the noise. Height
noise of ±1 mm, read at 1 kHz, is a rate of up to ±2 m/s from one sample to
the next. Times $K_d = 8$, that is a command that jumps by up to 16 m/s²
every millisecond.

Pid filters the measured rate with a first-order low-pass of time
constant $\tau$ (`derivative_tau`). The rate is then smooth, but late by
about $\tau$.

*Before you play:* the filtered run uses τ = 5 ms. How much smoother is its
command, and how much later does it settle?

The filter costs nothing while $\tau$ is small against the loop's own time
scale, about $1/K_d$ = 0.13 s here. At τ = 0.5 s the damping arrives so late
that the overshoot doubles, to 34 %. The cascade's rate loop, which runs
much faster, uses τ = 2 ms, a tenth of the motor's time constant.

## 5. Saturation, windup, and anti-windup

Every actuator has a limit. When the controller asks for more than the
actuator can give, the error stays large, and the integral keeps adding it
up. When the setpoint finally comes back within reach, the wound-up
integral holds the command at the limit until it has unwound. This is
integrator windup.

Pid prevents it by conditional integration. When the output is at a limit
and the error pushes further into it, the integral is frozen for that
step. When the error pulls back, it integrates as usual.

This section flies a different loop: a first-order plant, $T\dot x = -x + u$
with T = 0.1 s, such as a body rate behind a motor lag. Its actuator
saturates at $|u| \le 1$, so it can hold at most $x = 1$. Two PI
controllers fly two copies of it. One has its output limits set to the
actuator's, so its anti-windup engages. The other has its limits set wide
open, so its integral is never stopped, which is what a controller without
anti-windup does.

*Before you play:* the setpoint is 1.5 for 2 s, beyond the plant's reach,
and then 0.5. How long does each run take to get to 0.5?

The wound-up integral grows with the time spent at the limit, and so does
the recovery: about 1 s after a 0.5 s hold, and 3.5 s after a 3 s hold. The
anti-windup run reaches 0.5 in 0.09 s whatever the hold. The check under
the player is the closed-loop test's criterion: reaching the 2 % band
within 0.2 s of the drop.

A stage after the controller can also cut its command, as the
[mixer](/control/mixer/) does when it runs out of authority. Pid then
needs to be told, or its integral winds up behind that cut. The
[deployment guide](/control/pid-deployment/#external-saturation) shows it.
