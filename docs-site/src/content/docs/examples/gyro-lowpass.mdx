Flight controllers low-pass the gyro signal to strip motor and propeller
vibration before the rate loop sees it. This example builds that filter and
asks a hardware question: **which number format should it run in?**

## The signal

A gyro on a multirotor measures two things at once: the **body motion** the
controller cares about, and **vibration** from the motors and propellers, which
it does not. Here the motion is a 2 Hz roll rate (0.5 rad/s) and the vibration
a 180 Hz tone (0.3 rad/s), sampled at 1 kHz:

<SignalFigure
  svg={inputSvg}
  caption="200 ms around t = 1 s. The vibration is nearly as large as the motion and 90 times faster; fed straight into the rate controller, it would make the motors chase noise. Hover (or focus and use the arrow keys) for values."

## The filter

A 2nd-order Butterworth low-pass with cutoff $f_c = 30$ Hz at sample rate
$f_s = 1$ kHz, designed with the (prewarped) bilinear transform. The terms are
explained [below](#the-terms-explained):

$$
K = \tan\frac{\pi f_c}{f_s}, \qquad
H(z) = \frac{b_0 + b_1 z^{-1} + b_2 z^{-2}}{1 + a_1 z^{-1} + a_2 z^{-2}}
$$

realized in transposed direct form II as a 2-state state-space system with
MTL5 dense matrices:

$$
x_{k+1} = A x_k + B u_k, \qquad y_k = C x_k + D u_k
$$

In `double` the design meets its spec: $|H(2\,\text{Hz})| = 0.99999$, and the
180 Hz vibration is cut by **33.1 dB**, to about 2.2% of its amplitude:

## The price: delay

A filter cannot remove the vibration without also **delaying** the motion it
passes. Zoomed in on the moment the roll rate crosses zero:

<SignalFigure
  svg={delaySvg}
  caption="60 ms around the rising zero crossing at t = 1 s. The filter output crosses zero 7.5 ms after the true motion. The small wobble on the output is the residual 180 Hz ripple."
/>

From the exact coefficients, evaluating $H$ on the unit circle:

| Frequency | Gain $\lvert H \rvert$ | Phase | Phase delay | Group delay |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 Hz (body motion) | 0.99999 (−0.0 dB) | **−5.4°** | **7.49 ms** | 7.51 ms |
| 30 Hz (= $f_c$) | 0.7071 (−3.0 dB) | −90.0° | 8.33 ms | 7.55 ms |
| 180 Hz (vibration) | 0.0222 (−33.1 dB) | −167.8° | 2.59 ms | 0.24 ms |

- **Phase delay** is how far a sinusoid at that frequency is shifted in time,
  $-\angle H / (2\pi f)$. **Group delay** is how far the *envelope* of a narrow
  band around it is shifted, $-d\angle H / d\omega$. Across the passband the two
  agree, about 7.5 ms from DC up to $f_c$, so the motion comes out as a near-pure
  **time shift**, not a distortion.
- The test suite **measures** the lag (by projecting the filtered motion onto
  sine and cosine over three settled periods) and requires it to match the
  analytic phase delay to within 50 µs. Today they agree to the microsecond:
  7.491 ms.

**Why it matters.** The rate controller now sees the vehicle's motion 7.5 ms
late. Delay in a feedback loop costs phase margin: the controller corrects for
where the vehicle *was*. Lowering $f_c$ removes more vibration but adds more
delay. That is the central trade-off in gyro filtering, and the reason flight
controllers track the motor frequency with narrow **dynamic notch** filters
instead of reaching for an ever-lower low-pass.

## Number formats compared

The same filter runs in each format, and its output is compared with the
`double` reference (signal RMS 0.354 rad/s):

| Format | Bits | Error RMS (rad/s) | SNR |
|---|---|---|---|
| `float` | 32 | 5.5e-7 | 116.1 dB |
| `posit<32,2>` | 32 | 2.6e-8 | **142.8 dB** |
| IEEE half (`cfloat<16,5>`) | 16 | 5.0e-3 | 37.0 dB |
| Q3.12 fixed point (`fixpnt<16,12>`) | 16 | 3.3e-3 | 40.6 dB |
| `posit<16,1>` | 16 | 1.1e-3 | **49.9 dB** |

Identical under GCC 13 and Clang 18.

**Reading it.** A gyro signal lives near magnitude 1, where posits put most of
their precision (tapered accuracy). At 32 bits that makes `posit<32,2>` about
27 dB more accurate than `float`. At 16 bits, the width an embedded DSP path or
a narrow accelerator datapath would use, `posit<16,1>` beats both IEEE half
(+13 dB) and the classic Q-format fixed-point choice (+9 dB). Fixed point needs
the designer to pick the binary point; the posit needs no such tuning.

## The terms explained

**$f_s$, the sample rate** (1 kHz). How often the gyro is read. A sampled
signal can only represent frequencies up to the **Nyquist frequency**
$f_s/2$ = 500 Hz; everything in a digital filter's design lives on the interval
$0 \ldots f_s/2$.

**$f_c$, the cutoff frequency** (30 Hz). Where the filter's gain has fallen to
$1/\sqrt{2}$, i.e. −3 dB (half the power). Below $f_c$ the motion passes almost
unchanged. Above it the gain falls at 40 dB per decade, the slope of a 2nd-order
filter: −33 dB by 180 Hz. Butterworth is the choice of filter *shape*: the flattest
possible passband, with no ripple below $f_c$.

**$H(z)$, the transfer function.** The filter's input-to-output relation in the
$z$-domain. Each $z^{-1}$ means "one sample ago", so

$$
H(z) = \frac{b_0 + b_1 z^{-1} + b_2 z^{-2}}{1 + a_1 z^{-1} + a_2 z^{-2}}
$$

is a compact way of writing the **difference equation** the filter executes
every sample:

$$
y_k = b_0 u_k + b_1 u_{k-1} + b_2 u_{k-2} - a_1 y_{k-1} - a_2 y_{k-2}
$$

The $b$ coefficients weight recent inputs; the $a$ coefficients feed back
recent outputs, which is what makes this a recursive (IIR) filter, and what makes
it cheap: five multiplies per sample. Here $b_0 = b_2 = 0.00782$,
$b_1 = 0.01564$, $a_1 = -1.7347$, $a_2 = 0.7660$. Setting $z = e^{j 2\pi f / f_s}$,
a point on the unit circle, gives the frequency response: $|H|$ is the gain and
$\angle H$ the phase in the table above.

The example runs the same filter in **state-space form**. With two internal
states holding the "memory" of past samples (transposed direct form II):

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} -a_1 & 1 \\ -a_2 & 0 \end{bmatrix},\quad
B = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 - a_1 b_0 \\ b_2 - a_2 b_0 \end{bmatrix},\quad
C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix},\quad
D = b_0 .
$$

Same input-output behavior, written as matrix operations MTL5 can execute in
any number type.

**The bilinear transform and $K$.** Butterworth filters are defined in
continuous time (the analog $s$-domain). The **bilinear transform** turns an analog
design into a digital one by substituting

$$
s = 2 f_s \, \frac{1 - z^{-1}}{1 + z^{-1}} .
$$

This mapping is stable and exact in shape, but it squeezes the analog frequency
axis, which runs to infinity, into the digital interval $0 \ldots f_s/2$.
Frequencies are **warped**: analog frequency $\Omega$ and digital frequency $f$
are related by

$$
\Omega = 2 f_s \tan\frac{\pi f}{f_s} .
$$

Near 0 Hz the warping is negligible ($\tan x \approx x$). Toward Nyquist it
grows without bound. $K = \tan(\pi f_c / f_s)$ (= 0.0945 here) is this relation
evaluated at the cutoff: the cutoff as the *analog* prototype must see it.

**Prewarping.** Designing the analog prototype at the *warped* frequency, i.e.
using $K = \tan(\pi f_c/f_s)$ instead of the naive $\pi f_c / f_s$, so that after
the bilinear transform the digital filter's −3 dB point lands **exactly** on
$f_c$. Without prewarping, the cutoff drifts low, and the drift grows as
$f_c$ approaches Nyquist. At $f_s$ = 1 kHz:

| Designed $f_c$ | Actual −3 dB, prewarped | Actual −3 dB, not prewarped |
|---|---|---|
| 30 Hz (this example) | 30.0 Hz | 29.9 Hz (negligible) |
| 100 Hz | 100.0 Hz | 96.9 Hz |
| 250 Hz | 250.0 Hz | 211.9 Hz |

The same warping explains the stopband number. An analog 2nd-order Butterworth
gives about −31 dB at 6× $f_c$. Compressing the frequency axis toward Nyquist adds
attenuation up there, hence −33.1 dB at 180 Hz.

## Regenerating the figures

The figures are generated from the C++ example, so they show exactly what the
filter computes:

```bash
cmake --preset gcc-debug && cmake --build --preset gcc-debug
mkdir -p build/gyro-trace
REFLEX_DSP_TRACE_DIR=build/gyro-trace ./build/gcc-debug/tests/dsp_lowpass "*phase delay*"
node docs-site/scripts/gen-gyro-figures.mjs build/gyro-trace
```

The SVGs under `docs-site/src/figures/gyro-lowpass/` are committed; the docs
build doesn't run C++.

## MTL5 note

The filter's input enters as a 1-vector (`B * u` with `u` a `dense_vector`)
rather than `B * scalar`. MTL5's scalar×matrix and scalar×vector operators
currently accept only built-in arithmetic types
([stillwater-sc/mtl5#538](https://github.com/stillwater-sc/mtl5/issues/538)).
