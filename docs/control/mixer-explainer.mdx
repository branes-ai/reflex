The control loops ask for a wrench: a collective thrust and a torque about
each axis. A quadrotor has only four rotors, and each one can push between
zero and its maximum thrust. This page explains how
[`Mixer`](mixer.mdx) turns the wrench into four thrusts, and what it
gives up when the rotors cannot deliver all of it.

Every animation plays a trace recorded from the mixer's own C++, and the
scenario is linked under each player. The two widgets with sliders rerun
that C++, compiled to WebAssembly, in your browser. The vehicle is RP-1,
the [quadrotor model](../model/quadrotor.mdx)'s default: 1 kg, rotors 0.11 m
from the centre, and at most 12 N per rotor. The controls and keys are
described on the [Trace Player](../standards/trace-player.mdx#player-behaviour)
page.

## Four rotors, four demands

The rotors sit on the diagonals of a square, in PX4's Quad X order: 0 front
right, 1 rear left, 2 front left, 3 rear right. Rotors 0 and 1 spin
counter-clockwise seen from above, 2 and 3 clockwise. Each rotor's thrust
$T_i$ adds to the wrench in four ways:

| Wrench | T₀ front right | T₁ rear left | T₂ front left | T₃ rear right |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Collective F | + | + | + | + |
| Roll τx (left side up) | − | + | + | − |
| Pitch τy (nose down) | − | + | − | + |
| Yaw τz (counter-clockwise) | − | − | + | + |

Roll and pitch come from the thrust difference across the arms, with a
moment arm $a = L/\sqrt 2 = 0.078$ m. Yaw comes from the rotors' drag
torque, $k_m T$ with $k_m = 0.016$ m, which spins the frame the opposite
way to the rotor. The four rows are independent, so for any wrench there is
exactly one set of thrusts:

$$
T_i = \frac{F}{4} \pm \frac{\tau_x}{4a} \pm \frac{\tau_y}{4a} \pm \frac{\tau_z}{4k_m}
$$

For RP-1, 1 N·m of roll moves each rotor by 3.2 N, and 1 N·m of yaw by
15.6 N. Yaw is expensive: the drag arm is five times shorter than the
moment arm.

That formula is `allocate()`. It ignores the limits, so its thrusts can be
negative, or more than 12 N. `mix()` starts from it and keeps every rotor
in range.

## 1. When the rotors run out: what gives way first

When some rotor would need more than 12 N, or less than 0, something in the
demand has to give. `mix()` gives it up in a fixed order:

1. **yaw**, which turns the heading but does not move the vehicle;
2. then **collective thrust**, which changes the climb for a moment;
3. and **roll and pitch** last, scaled together so the tilt keeps its
   direction. The tilt is the direction of the thrust, so it decides where
   the vehicle goes.

In the torque plot the achieved roll follows its demand (dashed) until
3.74 s, where they part. Yaw parts from its demand first, at 0.9 s.

Why this order? Yaw is the cheapest to lose: a heading that lags for a
moment does not change the path. It is also the most expensive to keep,
because each N·m of yaw takes five times more thrust difference than roll or
pitch. A lost moment of collective changes the climb, which the loops can
make up. Losing roll and pitch means the thrust points somewhere other than
where the loops sent it. Scaling roll and pitch by one factor keeps the
direction of the tilt. The vehicle then still turns the right way, only more
slowly.

The torque one axis can get is set by the thrust range, not by the
collective: rotors 1 and 2 at 12 N against rotors 0 and 3 at 0 N gives
τx = 4a·6 N = 1.87 N·m. To get it, the collective has to sit in the
middle, at 24 N, which is where the mixer took it.

## 2. At the bottom of the throttle: airmode

The same thing happens at the other end. With the throttle near zero, the
rotors that should slow down cannot go below 0 N, so the mixer raises the
collective instead. It raises it only as far as roll and pitch need. The
collective ranks above yaw, so yaw gives way before the mixer lifts the
collective any further.

This is airmode: the vehicle keeps its attitude authority with the throttle
closed, in a dive or a flip. It also means the mixer spins the rotors up
whenever torque is asked for, even at zero throttle. A vehicle on the
ground, disarmed, or at motor idle must not reach the mixer: arming and idle
belong to the state machine around it.

Between the two ends the mixer does nothing at all: a demand that fits
comes back unchanged. Across each boundary its output changes smoothly,
because each step of the priority is a clamp of a bound that moves
smoothly with the demand. No rotor jumps when the mixer starts or stops
cutting.

## 3. Why not just clip each rotor?

The simplest mixer computes `allocate()` and clamps each rotor to its
range. That keeps every rotor in range, but the clamp removes thrust from
whichever rotors hit a limit, so the torque the four rotors make no longer
points the way the demand did. Some of the yaw comes out of the tilt, and
the collective drops by whatever was cut.

*Before you play:* two vehicles fly the same manoeuvre at full throttle: yaw
60° and roll 30° at once, with every rotor already asked for 12 N. One uses
`mix()`; the other (translucent) clips each rotor. Which one gets its tilt
right first, and what does each give up?

At mid throttle nothing saturates, and the two vehicles fly alike. The
mixer's order matters only at the edges, and that is where a racing drone
spends its hardest moments: punching out of a turn, or flipping with the
throttle closed. At 0 % throttle the clipped run's tilt is off by up to 12°
after the step, against 1° for `mix()`.

The difference is not that `mix()` delivers more. It is that what it gives
up is chosen, always in the same order, and reported. The cascade's
integrators read the report and stop integrating on the axes the mixer cut
(see the [cascade](cascade.mdx)), so they do not wind up behind it.
