This page explains how [`QuadrotorModel`](quadrotor.mdx) turns four
rotor speed commands into the motion of a rigid body. It builds the model up
one idea at a time: rotor, then thrust and torque, motor lag, attitude, and
translation. A last section shows a rigid-body effect that has nothing to do
with the rotors.

Every animation plays a trace recorded from the model's own C++ (the
scenario is linked under each player). Nothing on this page computes
dynamics in the browser. The controls, the keys, and the conventions are
described on the [Trace Player](../standards/trace-player.mdx#player-behaviour)
page.

## The vehicle

The model is a rigid body with four rotors in the PX4 Quad X layout. The
world frame has z up. The body frame has x forward, y left, and z up.

| Rotor | Position | Spin, seen from above |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | front right | counter-clockwise |
| 1 | rear left | counter-clockwise |
| 2 | front left | clockwise |
| 3 | rear right | clockwise |

The default parameters are RP-1's: 1 kg, an arm of 0.11 m, and a hover speed
of about 1660 rad/s per rotor. Each rotor can make up to 12 N, which it does
at ω_max ≈ 3672 rad/s.

## 1. Each rotor pushes and twists

A rotor spinning at ω makes a thrust $T = k_f\,\omega^2$ along body z. Since
the rotor sits off the centre of mass, the thrust also makes a torque about
body x and y. Spinning the air one way pushes the body the other way about z.
A counter-clockwise rotor makes a yaw torque of $-k_m T$.

*Before you play:* rotor 0 is front right. When it spins alone, which way
does the vehicle want to roll, and which way to pitch?

The torque is the model's own. The scenario reads it from `derivative()`
as J·ω̇ while the body rates are zero, so the gyroscopic term vanishes. A
grounded vehicle stays put while its vertical thrust is at most its weight,
which is why the vehicle does not move here. The
[ground contact](quadrotor-vv.mdx#ground-contact) animation on the V&V
page shows that rule at take-off and landing.

The four rotors give four independent controls: total thrust, and torque
about x, y, and z. The [mixer](../control/mixer.mdx) inverts this map.

## 2. Rotors lag their commands

A rotor cannot change speed at once. The model treats each motor as a
first-order lag with time constant τ = 20 ms:
$\dot\omega = (u - \omega)/\tau$, with the command u clamped to $[0, \omega_{max}]$.

*Before you play:* the command jumps by 10 %. How long until the thrust has
made most of that change?

The sliders rerun the scenario's C++, compiled to WebAssembly, in your
browser. With a slow motor the thrust arrives late and leaves late. At
τ = 60 ms the vehicle has gained 0.29 m/s by 0.25 s instead of 0.37 m/s,
and it makes up most of the difference after the command drops. At τ = 5 ms the check fails. The
motor is fine, but the 1 ms integration step is now a fifth of τ, and the
integrator's error passes the check's limit. A step size must stay small
against the fastest time constant, which is why `step()` requires dt ≤ τ.

The check under the player compares the recorded rotor speed with the
closed-form step response at every sample. An exact solution like this is
how the V&V plan verifies each term of the equations (BB-V1).

For a controller, the lag is the first thing it has to live with. A torque
commanded now arrives over the next few tens of milliseconds.

## 3. Torque turns the body, and tilted thrust moves it

Rotation and translation are coupled only through the direction of the
thrust. A roll torque does not move the vehicle sideways by itself. It
tilts the thrust vector, and the horizontal part of the tilted thrust
accelerates the vehicle.

*Before you play:* the left rotors speed up and the right rotors slow down,
then the reverse. Where does the vehicle end up, and does it stay level
afterwards?

The order of effects is the chain a controller works through in reverse:
rotor speed, then torque, roll rate, roll angle, and only then sideways
acceleration. A position controller therefore commands an attitude, and an
attitude controller commands rates, which is the
[cascade](../control/cascade.mdx) structure.

## 4. A free body that flips

The rate equation $\dot\omega = J^{-1}(\tau - \omega \times J\omega)$ has a
second term that does not come from the rotors. It is the gyroscopic term
of a rigid body, and it matters even with no torque at all. A body with
three different moments of inertia spins steadily about its largest and
its smallest axis. About the middle axis, any small disturbance grows, and
the body flips end over end (the intermediate-axis theorem, or
Dzhanibekov effect).

RP-1's inertia is (3, 3, 5)·10⁻³ kg·m². Its x and y moments are equal, so it
has no middle axis. This scenario uses (2, 4, 6)·10⁻³ kg·m² instead.

*Before you play:* the vehicle spins at 6 rad/s about body y, the middle
axis, and nothing pushes on it. Does the spin stay about y?

With the sliders, make y the largest or the smallest axis: the spin then
stays about y, and only wobbles. Only the middle axis is unstable.

The two flat plots are a check on the integrator as much as a fact of
physics. Classical RK4 does not conserve energy or momentum exactly. At
dt = 1 ms the drift over 5 s stays near 10⁻¹¹ of the initial values, which
the check under the player bounds (BB-V3). The motion is also sensitive to
roundoff near the flip. A 10⁻¹⁵ rad/s change in the initial rate moves the
trace by about 10⁻¹¹ rad/s, which is still well inside the tolerance the
docs CI uses to compare a regenerated trace with this one.
